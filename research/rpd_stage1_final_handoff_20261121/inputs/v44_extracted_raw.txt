When Does Diagnostic Interaction Compromise
Robot Recovery?
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review
Abstract—A diagnostic action changes both what an au-
tonomous robot knows and which task continuations remain pos-
sible. An informative contact may preserve conventional motion
constraints yet remove an otherwise executable correction. We
study this coupling in physics-based robotic simulation through
post-diagnosis recoverability, the set of fixed feedback corrections
that can complete the task within the remaining deadline. Our
methodology combines a physical belief update, a response-
contingent recovery decision, and matched probe-versus-hold
interventions that distinguish contact-induced option loss from
elapsed time. We formulate two quantitative hypotheses: in-
formative probing causes materially frequent recovery-option
erasure, and modeling its consequences improves task completion
relative to constrained information seeking. An exact finite-world
illustration demonstrates the conflict; a fully specified contact-
rich assembly simulation study defines the probes, matched
controls, recovery policies, and statistical tests needed to establish
its prevalence and consequences. The result is a testable method-
ology for judging diagnosis by both information gained and task
recovery retained.
I. INTRODUCTION
Consider a simulated robot attempting an insertion when
contact contradicts its expected alignment. A small physical
probe can reveal an obstruction, but it can also wedge the
part more tightly. Even when the probe stays inside the
allowed workspace and contact limits, the robot may have
fewer successful ways to finish. Contact-rich assembly makes
this coupling between sensing and action particularly conse-
quential [7, 8].
Active perception uses action to improve observations [1],
while safe active diagnosis limits exploratory motion [5].
Task-aware tactile disambiguation stops when pose hypotheses
share an executable grasp [9]; task-compatible active calibra-
tion studies whether identification motions retain downstream
controllability [4]. Reversibility-aware learning values return
paths [3]. Our recovery-specific estimand instead isolates what
a diagnostic probe changes after a contact failure: which
corrective policies cease to work relative to holding for the
same time? This matched comparison isolates the physical
consequence of diagnosis from mere delay and makes it
possible to evaluate task-relevant information together with
subsequent task capability.
Even a constraint-compliant probe may make completion
impossible with the robot’s current corrective skills. If com-
mon, this effect makes information-only diagnosis costly; if
rare, a matched-hold comparison can delimit when simpler
diagnosis suffices. Figure 1 motivates our question: when does
informative probing destroy recoverable continuations, and
when does anticipating this loss improve completion?

We develop a unified recoverability-preserving diagnosis
(RPD) methodology. It (i) defines success relative to a task,
feedback-policy repertoire and deadline; (ii) measures probe-
induced losses against a time-matched physical hold; and (iii)
compares diagnostic decisions made with and without pre-
dicted recovery consequences. Response-contingent planning
provides the decision mechanism [6]; the central scientific
object is the recovery-specific effect measured by the matched
intervention and its consequence for diagnostic choice.
H1, informative option erasure. For a frozen constrained
information-gain (IG) selector, physical probes erase all cor-
rections retained after an equal-duration hold in more than
10% of predecision-eligible simulated assembly checkpoints,
counting direct action and abstention in the denominator. In
over half of those erasure cases, the probe increases the held-
out likelihood of the original contact mode by more than 20%
over both the pre-probe and hold-based predictors.
H2, decision consequence. With identical simulated ob-
servations, models, controls and time budgets, recoverability-
aware selection improves terminal completion by more than
five percentage points over constrained information-gain se-
lection, while increasing the measured constraint-violation
rate by less than two points. The thresholds represent one
materially compromised recovery per ten eligible attempts and
one additional completion per twenty; they are prospective pre-
dictions, not measured effects. We expect contact-dependent
losses near clearance-scale probing, not universal damage from
information gathering.

B. Was the probe genuinely informative?
Let Z denote the source-assigned pre-probe contact mode,
X the pre-probe observable features, and Yp, YH probe and
hold responses. On independent discovery data, freeze three
equally trained predictive distributions: f0(Z | X), fp(Z |
X, Yp), and fH(Z | X, YH). Their held-out log-score differ-

D0 = log fp(Z) − log f0(Z),
DH = log fp(Z) − log fH(Z).
(6)

probability from 1/3 to above 0.4. The majority criterion

IV. PROPOSED SIMULATION STUDY: CONTACT-RICH
ASSEMBLY
A. Simulation interface and independent sampling
All proposed experiments run exclusively in physics simu-
lation, without a hardware robot. The primary setting is lateral
peg insertion in ManiSkill3/SAPIEN [8]. Peg and fixture
geometry share a known 3 mm nominal per-side clearance
c; a fixed generator introduces three equally probable hidden
modes: transverse offset in y, transverse offset in z, or angular
misalignment. Each mode includes both signs and bounded
within-mode variation. The checkpoint occurs after a closed
grasp and a short contact-making approach; eligibility uses
only predecision observations, persistent contact and measured
controller health. No outcome of the candidate probes or
recovery policies enters eligibility. Prevalence therefore refers
to this specified checkpoint distribution, not all manipulation
failures; amplitude-response analysis and a later, separately
sampled contact-task replication test how the phenomenon
depends on this choice.
The source geometry uses square peg-hole clearance c =
3 mm and a fixed fixture frame. Hidden offset signs are
symmetric and straddle clearance, perturbing peg-in-gripper
alignment while the controller retains its nominal calibration.
True mode, offset and pose are reserved for evaluation; probe
choice never receives them.
The actions are fixed before the confirmation cohort. Over
d = 0.5 s at 20 Hz, hold retains commanded equilibrium,
preload commands an axial displacement of c/2, and dither
commands one transverse sinusoidal cycle of amplitude c, all
with the same gripper and position-control parameters. Two
feedback recovery policies follow: a0 continues the insertion
from a nominal peg-to-tool transform; a1 withdraws by 4c,
realigns using a predecision transform estimate, and reinserts.
The root-fitted estimate is fixed across every matched branch;
probe responses select the policy, not new privileged execution
parameters.
Online input consists of arm proprioception, calibrated tool
pose, commanded equilibrium and known fixture dimensions.
A 10-sample diagnostic response adds only these channels.
True peg pose, hidden fault mode, contact forces and al-
ternative outcomes are isolated to evaluation. Both policies
receive τ − d = 3.5 s after intervention; direct recovery has
the full τ = 4 s. No simulator state is reset between probe and
correction. The primary goal is stable mid-depth insertion held
for 0.25 s within prescribed translation, rotation and velocity
tolerances; the native head-position flag is separately reported.
Continuous sampled checks cover joint limits, joint speed,
tracking residual, object slip, and peg-fixture contact-force
envelopes. An unsafe path remains a measured violation, not
an excluded result.
Both recovery macros use the same feedback controller and
stored predecision estimate. Success requires a stable insertion
dwell, not just the native head-position flag; the full simulated
path is checked for contact force, object slip, tracking and joint
limits. This is a measured simulator outcome, not a hardware
safety guarantee.
B. Frozen model and fair comparisons
Discovery-only data fit a nominal grasp-transform estimator
and a shared finite probe-response/outcome model. Refer-
ence particles have root feature X, probe response Yp, and
matched recovery labels; Gaussian distance weights and a
frozen nearest-response codebook define both policies’ pos-
terior predictions. The RPD decision uses Eq. (4); IG maxi-
mizes predicted contact-mode information, subject to the same
estimated pre-recovery violation budget of .02. The direct and
STOP options use identical goal and timing definitions. The
model receives no true pose, latent mode or paired evaluator
outcome during online decision making.
Predecision features are joint positions/velocities, calibrated
tool pose, tool command and known peg dimensions. Ten
time samples of the same 23 scalar channels form a 230-
dimensional probe response. Ground-truth forces and hidden
mode remain evaluator-only; shared discovery-fitted scaling
and response models give both selectors identical inputs.
Prediction error is reported separately from nominal planning
optimality.
From fixed candidate lists, the study collects 64 discov-
ery and 1,024 confirmation parents (candidate caps 128 and
2,560), with eligibility determined solely from pre-probe con-
tact, grasp and controller-health measurements. A separate 12-
parent source qualification must yield at least eight eligible
roots, six post-hold recoverable roots, and replay-consistent
controller and timing traces. A failed source gate blocks
confirmation and triggers separately recorded development, not
substitution of favorable seeds. Each confirmed parent supplies
hold, preload and dither followed by both correction policies:
6,144 matched simulated continuations, plus direct-action con-
trols. Every raw candidate, exclusion and missing branch is
counted; the independent unit is the original checkpoint parent.
C. Statistical decisions and scientific outcomes
For H1, one-sided exact Clopper–Pearson (CP) lower
bounds [2] test qIG > .10 on all eligible parents and ηγ > .50
on the random number nJ of prespecified erasure cases,
each at level .025. This directly tests whether diagnostic
improvement co-occurs with option loss in the same cases.
We report h, the fraction retaining a post-hold correction, and
qIG/h, the erasure prevalence conditional on that event when
h > 0. A shuffled-response control tests whether score gains
actually require the measured probe signal.
For H2, count paired RPD-only successes n+ and IG-only
successes n−, with m = n+ + n− and r = P(URPD ̸= UIG).
Defining θ = P(URPD = 1 | URPD ̸= UIG) gives ∆U =
r(2θ − 1). Independent one-sided CP bounds for r and θ,
each allocated .0125, yield a valid 97.5% lower limit on ∆U :
multiply 2L(θ) − 1 by L(r) when nonnegative and by U(r)
otherwise; for m = 0, use −U(r).
For whole-path violations, write v+ and v− for RPD-only
and IG-only violations, mC = v+ +v− and ϵC = .02. We test
∆C < ϵC directly with a paired trinomial score:

A pre-specified longer deadline and a third pre-specified
simulated correction (withdraw, reorient, realign, reinsert)
test repertoire and budget sensitivity without changing the
primary two-skill comparison. Three frozen probe intensities
(c/2, c, 3c/2) assess dose response on a disjoint development
cohort, rather than choosing a favorable confirmation intensity.
The two fixed-probe erasure rates and their original-contact-
mode strata are also reported on the untouched confirmation
cohort, with no threshold changes or selected-case exclusions.
In all outcome regimes we report the exact lower and upper
bounds on qIG, the hold-recoverable fraction h, conditional
erasure qIG/h when h > 0, and the dose-response curve. A
confidence interval spanning .10 is inconclusive, whereas an
upper bound below .10 limits H1 in the declared population.
Source or sensing failure prevents a physical conclusion; H1
without H2 establishes an effect without extra policy benefit;
H2 without H1 motivates analysis of partial-option losses.
All alternatives are evaluated under the same state recon-
struction and held-out parent distribution. A second contact-
manipulation mechanism is reserved for later validation, not
needed to define the method.
At 1,024 parents, zero observed erasures bound qIG above
by 0.360% (one-sided 97.5% exact CP); at least 123 erasures
are needed to certify qIG > .10. Under hypothetical rates
qIG = .15, ηγ = .80, H1 joint power is 99.7%. For H2,
20% completion discordance with a 12-point gain and 2.5%
violation discordance with zero difference give 99.3% and
96.4% respective test powers, hence at least 95.6% joint power
regardless of dependence. These are design calculations, not
robot outcomes.
A later simulation-only replication can examine cluttered
target extraction, where a short contact displacement changes
accessibility or support before a fixed grasp/extraction correc-
tion. It retains the matched hold, recovery-set evaluation and
independent-parent analysis in simulation.

V. ANALYTICAL ILLUSTRATION AND SCIENTIFIC
IMPLICATIONS
Consider three equiprobable contact states A, B, C and two
fixed corrections. A fine diagnostic probe identifies the state
exactly (log 3 ≈ 1.099 nats), while a coarse probe distin-
guishes {A, B} from C (0.637 nats). The coarse response
suffices to select a successful correction. The finer probe,
despite perfectly identifying C, changes its physical state until
neither correction succeeds. Thus maximizing information
can reduce task completion even when a response-dependent
correction is available.
A constructed finite-world sweep varies the intensity of
this interaction (Table II). As intensity increases, its predicted
diagnostic information remains useful while the fine probe
causes more unrecoverable continuations. The task-aware pol-
icy chooses the coarse probe in the affected worlds, and a
general contingent planner with the same terminal objective
makes identical choices. The sweep is analytical evidence of
a mechanism and its decision consequence, not a measured
SAPIEN dose response or an H1 population estimate.
What makes the hypotheses informative? H1 predicts
more than a single harmful contact: within the fixed source
distribution, IG-selected diagnosis must erase an entire hold-
retained correction repertoire often enough to matter, and a
majority of those cases must exhibit a material true-mode
signal. The underlying physical quantities qp for each fixed
probe and h clarify the mechanism. A large qp with small
qIG indicates that the selector avoids disruptive probes; small
qp despite high probe-selection frequency indicates that this
regime preserves recovery. Neither can be inferred from the
single aggregate rate alone. The three fixed amplitudes and
predecision contact-mode strata assess where the interaction is
consequential, without selecting a favorable evaluation subset.
H2 asks a separate question: whether accounting for op-
tion loss improves completion beyond what an identical
information-seeking model achieves, without materially in-
creasing whole-path violations. A full-objective planner or
task-consensus rule may match RPD; the matched comparison
isolates the value of the recovery objective, not a claimed
advantage of the search algorithm. If H1 holds and H2 does
not, the causal mechanism may be real but already managed
by task consensus, or model prediction error may dominate
planning. The response-blind and direct-recovery controls dis-
tinguish these interpretations.
Scientific conclusions across outcomes. Exact bounds
on qIG, the hold-recoverable fraction h, fixed-probe rates
qp, constraint compliance and model prediction error are
reported together. A small upper bound on erasure in a well-
qualified simulation population would delimit when simpler
information-seeking diagnosis is adequate; a wide interval or
an unqualified source would remain unresolved, not establish
absence of the phenomenon. The second deadline and third
correction determine whether measured losses reflect time
limits or the declared skill repertoire. A later clutter-extraction
replication checks whether the same measured contrast extends
to a different contact mechanism.
RPD therefore turns the effect of physical diagnosis on
future executable corrections into a measurable, task-specific
scientific question. The proposed simulation study can es-
tablish where acquiring information and preserving recovery
conflict, or bound how often they conflict under known con-
straints. In either case, the conclusion concerns what diagnosis
leaves the robot able to do, not information quality in isola-
tion.
